# Your 'Why' Is the Only Thing That Will Keep You Dialling After Rejection — Here's How to Find It

## The Moment the Will Disappears

Here's something most sales training never tells you: when you stop performing, your skills haven't gone anywhere. You still know how to open a conversation, how to handle an objection, how to read a room. The technique is intact. What's gone is something quieter and far more essential — the will to pick up the phone again.

I learned this the hard way early in my sales career, during a stint running roadshow signups for a bank credit card. The card was free. No annual fee, good rewards, nothing to lose for the customer. I genuinely believed this would be straightforward. I was wrong. Three days in, after approaching somewhere between 250 and 300 people each day, I had closed exactly three signups. One day I closed none. I stood in that shopping mall, watched strangers walk past me in both directions, and felt something I didn't have a name for yet — not failure exactly, but a kind of hollowing out. My skills were fine. My will was flickering.

That experience gave me something worth more than any sales technique I've picked up since. It taught me that there are two things you need to stay in this profession: the ability to sell, and the reason you show up to sell at all. Training sharpens the first. Only purpose protects the second.

## Why 'Earning More' Isn't Enough of a Why

Most people, when they enter sales, have a reason. The income potential. The freedom. A fresh start. These are real motivations, and there's nothing wrong with them. But when rejection stacks up — and it always does, because sales is a numbers game by nature — a surface-level 'why' tends to dissolve exactly when you need it most.

Think about what actually happens inside you when you hear a string of no's. It isn't just disappointment. It's a quiet internal narrative that starts to build: maybe this isn't for me, maybe I'm not cut out for this, maybe I should stop. That voice doesn't respond to commission structures. It doesn't care how good the product is. It only quiets when you're anchored to something deeper than the deal.

I think about a close friend of mine — a financial planner who has qualified for the Million Dollar Round Table eighteen times, while raising two young boys as a single father and running two other businesses alongside. I once asked him how he found the time and drive for all of it. His answer was simple: once you know why you want to do something, and that why is strong enough, the time you need will show up. Without a strong why, he said, time will always be claimed by other things.

That's not motivational language. That's a precise description of how prioritisation actually works. We make time for what feels important. And something only feels important when we understand, at a bone-deep level, why it matters.

The mistake most people make is stopping at the first answer. You ask yourself why you're in sales and you say, 'to earn a good living.' Fine. But ask again: why does that matter? And again after the answer to that. Push through five layers, or ten, and you'll find something that financial pressure alone can't erode — the image of your children's future, the freedom to care for someone you love, the proof to yourself that you're capable of building something from nothing. That is what keeps you dialling after the third consecutive no on a Thursday afternoon.

## Rejection as Data, Not Verdict

One of the most important reframes I've ever encountered came from a conversation with my mentor Ron Kaufman. We were talking about how people in sales experience rejection, and he made a distinction I haven't forgotten. There's a popular idea about building thick skin, he said — but that starts from the assumption that rejection is an attack you have to armour yourself against. And there's another phrase we all use: bounce back. But that too implies you've already been knocked down and now have to recover, return to where you were before.

What if neither of those is quite right? What if the goal isn't to endure rejection or recover from it, but to move forward through it — to let it feed you rather than deplete you?

He described it as a flame. Your motivation, your drive, your will to keep showing up — that's the flame. Traditional resilience says, don't let rejection blow it out. But a deeper kind of resilience asks: what if each no became fuel? What if hearing it made the flame burn more steadily, not less?

This is what I mean when I talk about The Sales-Rejection Paradox. The more people you present to, the more chances you have to close — and the more rejections you'll receive. These two things are inseparable. When you stop fearing the no, when you start reading it as information rather than judgment, the whole equation shifts. A no tells you something: about timing, about fit, about whether you're presenting to the right person. It's not a verdict on your worth. It's a data point. And data, unlike defeat, is useful.

The salesperson who internalises this doesn't need to white-knuckle their way through a rough week. They simply keep going — not because they're impervious to discouragement, but because their why gives every no a context that doesn't swallow them whole.

## The Flame Is Yours to Tend

Here's what I've come to believe after years of coaching salespeople, writing about resilience, and sitting with my own hard patches: motivation is not a renewable resource that replenishes automatically. It's more like a flame that requires deliberate, consistent tending. When you neglect it — when you don't revisit your purpose, when you let the small erosions of daily rejection accumulate without processing them — it dims. And once it goes out entirely, it doesn't simply reignite on its own.

This is why habits matter more than inspiration. Inspiration is weather. Habits are the shelter. A short daily practice of returning to your why — writing it down, sitting with it, asking yourself what you're really building toward — does something that no motivational talk can do sustainably on its own. It keeps the flame at a steady, workable height even when the results aren't there yet.

I spent two years writing my book on this subject, often in the corner of a Starbucks between other commitments, in cities I happened to be passing through. There was no perfect setting. There was just the habit of returning to the work, one small effort at a time, because I knew why it mattered. Sales is no different. The discipline of reconnecting with your purpose isn't a soft practice. It is, I'd argue, the most practical thing you can do in a profession where your emotional state directly determines your performance.

So if you're in a rough stretch — if the pipeline feels thin and the no's are loud and the will to keep going is quieter than you'd like — don't reach first for a new technique. Ask instead whether you still know, clearly and viscerally, why you're doing this at all. The answer to that question is what keeps you in the game long enough for the skills to matter.
