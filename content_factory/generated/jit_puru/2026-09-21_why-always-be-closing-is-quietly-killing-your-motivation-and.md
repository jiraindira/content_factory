# Why 'Always Be Closing' Is Quietly Killing Your Motivation (And What to Do Instead)

## The Advice That Sounds Like Winning But Feels Like Losing

There's a moment most salespeople recognise — standing in front of a prospect, mentally rehearsing the close before the conversation has even begun. You're listening, but not really. You're scanning for the opening, the signal, the right moment to steer toward yes. It feels like discipline. It feels like you're doing what you were trained to do.

But somewhere underneath that performance, something quietly hollows out.

I've spoken with hundreds of people in sales — financial planners, insurance agents, real estate professionals, direct sellers — and the pattern that emerges isn't a skill problem. It's a flame problem. The relentless pressure of Always Be Closing doesn't just make prospects uncomfortable. Over time, it makes *you* uncomfortable. It corrodes the very thing that got you into the game: the genuine desire to help someone, to connect with them, to find a solution that actually fits their life.

When closing becomes the only goal, every conversation becomes a transaction. And transactional energy is exhausting to carry — because it means every rejection lands not as data, but as defeat.

## What ABC Does to Your Inner World

I remember sitting in an insurance recruitment seminar at seventeen. The agency director said something that stopped me cold: "Once you become an agent, everyone you know is a prospect. Start with your closest friends and family." I was young and ambitious, genuinely drawn to the opportunity. But something about those words landed wrong. Not because the intention was malicious, but because it reframed every human relationship as a potential transaction. It turned people into targets.

I didn't join. And that instinct — that discomfort with seeing every interaction as a deal to be closed — has shaped everything I've come to believe about what actually sustains a long career in sales.

Here's what Always Be Closing quietly does: it positions rejection as failure. If every conversation is aimed at a close, then every no is a miss. And when you string together enough misses, the math becomes personal. You stop thinking "that prospect wasn't the right fit" and start thinking "I'm not good enough." The rejection stops being information and starts being a verdict.

This is where motivation dies — not dramatically, but slowly. Like a candle in a draught. A conversation with my mentor Ron Kaufman gave me the image that has stayed with me ever since. He described motivation as a flame inside us — our will to act, to serve, to try again. It's real and it's powerful, but it's also delicate. The big dramatic rejections don't usually extinguish it. It's the accumulation of smaller ones, the ignored follow-ups, the self-doubt after a bad week, the quiet erosion of a mindset trained to close rather than connect. Once that flame goes out, it doesn't reignite on its own.

Always Be Closing speeds up that erosion, because it puts you in conflict with two things that are simply true about how people work. First, people love to buy, but they hate being sold to. The moment a prospect senses they're being steered rather than heard, their defences go up. Second, people buy into *you* before they buy your product. Trust and genuine connection are not soft extras — they are the deal. When your entire posture is oriented toward closing, both of these truths work against you.

## The Shift That Changes Everything

What I've found — both in my own experience moving into sales at thirty with no clear credentials and in the years since working with professionals across industries — is that the most consistently successful people in sales aren't the most aggressive closers. They're the ones who've genuinely internised that their function is to help the customer, not to fulfil their own targets.

This isn't idealism. It's strategy. When your real goal in a conversation is to understand whether what you offer will genuinely serve this person, something in the dynamic shifts. You ask better questions. You listen differently. You're willing to say "I don't think this is the right fit for you" — and that honesty builds more trust than any closing technique ever could. The ones who say yes when you work this way tend to become long-term clients. The ones who say no often remember how you treated them and refer someone who's a better match.

The four-step sales cycle is actually simple: find something worth selling, find people who might need it, tell them about it, ask if they want it. What makes it feel hard is the emotional weight we attach to the fourth step. We treat the ask as the whole game, when in truth the quality of everything before it determines the answer. Shift your energy toward the first three steps — toward genuine prospecting, real storytelling, and authentic connection — and the close becomes less of a performance and more of a natural conclusion.

This also changes your relationship with rejection. If your goal was always to help this specific person, then a no simply means this wasn't the right match. It's not personal. It's not a verdict. It's information that points you toward someone who is a better fit. Each rejection, understood this way, is genuinely one step closer to the right yes — not as a motivational cliché, but as a logical truth about how probability works in sales.

## What Keeps the Flame Burning

Motivation is not a feeling you wait for. It's something you tend to, the way you tend to a flame. And the practices that tend to it aren't complicated, but they require intention.

Knowing your why — the real one, the one underneath the surface goal — is what carries you through the stretches where results are slow and the rejections feel heavy. It's not enough to know you want to hit a target or earn a commission. What matters is what that means for your life, for the people you're responsible for, for the version of yourself you're working toward. A strong purpose makes the inevitable setbacks feel like part of the journey rather than evidence that you should stop.

Self-talk matters more than most people admit. The story you tell yourself after a difficult conversation becomes the lens through which you approach the next one. Negative spirals are real, and they compound. But so does the opposite. Choosing how you interpret a rejection — as feedback rather than failure, as redirection rather than rejection of your worth — is a practice that builds over time. It doesn't come naturally at first, but it becomes more fluent the more deliberately you engage with it.

And habits, more than motivation, are what actually carry you through the hard stretches. The morning routines, the end-of-day reflections, the consistent small actions that keep you in motion even when enthusiasm is low — these are the scaffolding that holds the flame in place when the wind picks up.

The paradox at the heart of sales is this: the more people you speak to, the more rejections you'll face — and the higher your probability of success. Rejection isn't the obstacle to your goal. It's part of the path. The only question worth sitting with is this: when the next no arrives, will you let it extinguish your flame, or will you learn to let it feed it?
